Most first products don't fail because the code was bad. They fail because the team built the wrong thing, built too much of it, or ran out of money before learning what customers actually wanted. A well-scoped MVP (minimum viable product) is the best protection against all three.

This guide walks through a practical way to scope an MVP, whether you're a nontechnical founder or a product lead inside an established business.

## Start with the problem, not the feature list

It's tempting to begin with a list of screens and features. Resist that for a moment and write down three things first:

1. **Who has the problem?** Be specific. "Small businesses" is too broad; "owners of independent dental practices with two to five chairs" is something you can design for.
2. **What is the problem, in their words?** If you've talked to potential customers, use the phrases they used. If you haven't, that's your first task.
3. **How are they solving it today?** Spreadsheets, a competitor, a manual process, or nothing at all. Your MVP has to be meaningfully better than that alternative for at least one important job.

If you can't answer these clearly, more engineering won't help yet. A few customer conversations will.

## Define what "viable" means for you

"Minimum" gets most of the attention, but "viable" is where scoping decisions are really made. Viable means the product is good enough that the people you're targeting will genuinely use it to do the job — and that you can learn something from their behavior.

Write down what you want to learn from the first release. For example:

- Will practice managers actually use online booking instead of the phone?
- Will customers pay a monthly subscription for this, and at roughly what price?
- Can our operations team process requests faster with this tool than without it?

Each of these learning goals points to the features that matter and, just as importantly, the features that don't.

## Map the core journey

Next, sketch the single most important path through your product, from the moment someone arrives to the moment they get value. Keep it to a handful of steps.

For a booking product, that might be: find a time → enter details → confirm → receive a reminder → show up. Everything that directly supports that journey is a candidate for the MVP. Everything else goes on a "later" list.

A simple test for each feature: **if we removed this, could someone still complete the core journey and get value?** If the answer is yes, it probably isn't MVP scope.

## Cut scope deliberately — and write it down

Scope rarely shrinks by accident. It helps to sort every idea into one of three groups:

| Group | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Must have | The core journey breaks without it | Confirming a booking |
| Should have | Valuable, but there's a workaround for now | Rescheduling (staff can do it manually at first) |
| Later | Nice ideas that don't serve the first learning goal | Loyalty points, multi-location support |

The "later" list is not a graveyard. It's a record of good ideas you're choosing to postpone, which makes it much easier to say no today without losing anything.

## Use manual work where it's cheaper than code

Some of the most effective MVPs quietly rely on people behind the scenes. An admin approving sign-ups by hand, a weekly report built in a spreadsheet, or a support inbox instead of a help center can all be fine for the first release.

Automate a process once you've confirmed people need it and you understand it well enough to build it right. Code is expensive to change; a manual process is not.

## Don't skip the unglamorous essentials

Cutting scope doesn't mean cutting corners. Even a small first release should include:

- **Security basics** — proper authentication, sensible access controls, and careful handling of personal data.
- **Accessibility** — usable with a keyboard and screen reader, readable contrast, and clear form errors.
- **Basic analytics or feedback** — some way to learn whether people complete the core journey (with appropriate consent and privacy practices).
- **A way to deploy changes safely** — so you can fix and improve quickly after launch.

These are far cheaper to build in from the start than to retrofit.

## Estimate with ranges and assumptions

Once the scope is clear, an experienced team can break it into pieces and estimate each one. Be wary of a single precise number this early. A useful estimate is a range, along with the assumptions it depends on — for example, "assumes one payment provider, English only, and web first."

When assumptions change, the estimate should change with them, and you should hear about it right away.

## Plan what happens after launch

An MVP is the beginning of a learning loop, not a finish line. Before you launch, decide:

- What you'll measure in the first four to eight weeks.
- How you'll gather qualitative feedback (calls, interviews, support conversations).
- Roughly how much budget and time you're keeping in reserve for the next iteration.

Teams that budget for the second release tend to get much more value from the first.

## A quick checklist

Before you commit to building, you should be able to say yes to each of these:

- We can describe the target user and their problem in one or two sentences.
- We know what we want to learn from the first release.
- We have a core journey of a handful of steps.
- Every MVP feature is necessary for that journey.
- We have a written "later" list.
- We have an estimate range with clear assumptions.
- We've reserved budget for learning and iteration after launch.
