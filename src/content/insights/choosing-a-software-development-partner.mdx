Choosing who builds your software is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make for a digital product. The right partner helps you make good decisions, not just write code. The wrong one can cost months and a significant share of your budget before the problems become obvious.

Here's a practical guide to evaluating development partners — agencies, studios, and freelancers alike.

## Get clear on what you need first

Before you talk to anyone, write a short brief. It doesn't need to be technical. Cover:

- **The problem** you're solving and who you're solving it for.
- **What exists today** — designs, code, data, tools, or nothing yet.
- **Your constraints** — budget range, timeline, and anything fixed (a launch event, a compliance requirement, an integration you can't avoid).
- **What you need from a partner** — strategy and design, engineering only, ongoing capacity, or a mix.

A clear brief makes conversations more productive and makes proposals easier to compare.

## Look for how they think, not just what they've built

Portfolios are useful, but they only show finished results. In early conversations, pay attention to the questions a potential partner asks:

- Do they ask about your users and business goals, or jump straight to technology?
- Do they push back thoughtfully when something seems risky or unnecessary?
- Do they suggest ways to reduce scope or validate ideas before building them?

A partner who asks good questions before a contract is signed is likely to keep asking them during the project.

## Ask for evidence you can verify

Any partner can describe themselves well. Ask for things you can check:

- **Relevant work** they're able to share, and what their role was specifically.
- **References** you can speak with directly, ideally from projects similar in size to yours.
- **Code or technical samples**, if you have someone who can review them.

Newer firms may have less to show, and that's not automatically a problem — everyone starts somewhere. What matters is that they're transparent about their experience and don't overstate it.

## Understand how they'll work with you

The day-to-day working relationship matters as much as technical skill. Ask:

- Who will actually work on my project, and who is my main point of contact?
- How often will I see working software?
- How do you report progress, and what happens when something slips?
- How do you handle changes to scope?
- What hours do you work, and how much overlap will we have?

Listen for specific answers. "We're very agile" is not an answer; "you'll get a demo every two weeks and a written update every Friday" is.

## Read the proposal carefully

A strong proposal should make the following clear:

| Area | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | What's included — and explicitly what isn't |
| Estimate | A range with stated assumptions, not just a single number |
| Milestones | Concrete checkpoints with something you can see or test |
| Team | Roles involved and roughly how much of their time |
| Process | How work is planned, reviewed, tested, and deployed |
| Change handling | How new requests are estimated and approved |

Be cautious of proposals that are vague about scope but very precise about price, or that promise unusually fast timelines without explaining how.

## Make sure you'll own what you pay for

Clarify ownership and access before you start:

- **Intellectual property** — the contract should clearly state when and how ownership of code and work product transfers to you.
- **Repositories and accounts** — ideally, code lives in a repository your company controls, and cloud services are set up under your accounts.
- **Documentation and handover** — what you'll receive so another team could continue the work if needed.

Have a qualified attorney review the agreement, especially the IP, confidentiality, liability, and termination sections.

## Watch for red flags

A few warning signs worth taking seriously:

- Guarantees about outcomes that no one can honestly promise.
- Reluctance to put the team, process, or ownership terms in writing.
- No discussion of testing, security, or accessibility.
- Pressure to sign quickly or pay a large amount upfront before any discovery.
- Testimonials or client logos that can't be verified.

## Start small if you can

If you're unsure, consider beginning with a short, paid discovery or prototyping phase. It's a low-risk way to see how a partner communicates, how they think, and the quality of what they deliver — before committing to a larger build.

## A quick evaluation checklist

- They asked thoughtful questions about our users and goals.
- They were honest about their experience and what they don't know.
- We know who will work on the project and how we'll communicate.
- The proposal has clear scope, assumptions, and milestones.
- Ownership of code and accounts is clearly defined in writing.
- We've spoken to references or reviewed work we can verify.

We'd encourage you to use this checklist with every partner you evaluate — including us.
